Title of reading selection: Computational co-creation in digital media art: adaptive aesthetic intelligence for interactive visual expression




___In their 2026 Journal of Big Data article, “Computational co-creation in digital media art: adaptive aesthetic intelligence for interactive visual expression,” Liu, Bai, and Li present an Adaptive Aesthetic Intelligence (AAI) framework. It combines variational autoencoders (VAEs) with attention-based neural style modulation, allowing digital artworks to change in real time in response to user input. The authors contend that most current AI art tools are “passive generators” that restrict genuine co-creativity, so they cast AAI as a creative partner instead of a mere instrument.

The system is trained on a Kaggle dataset blending human-made and AI-generated images, and it reduces each image to a 128-dimensional latent representation, which the authors credit with lowering computational demands and holding inference to about 18-25 milliseconds per frame. My claim is that the paper offers a persuasive conceptual argument for interactive, user-in-the-loop AI art and introduces an intriguing stabilization method, yet its main results rest on weaker evidence than its assured tone implies. The most solid support comes from the latent-space analysis: the baseline update rule drifts to a peak norm of 60.77, whereas the radial feedback design remains bounded near 8.90 and cuts mean acceleration from 3.397 to 1.100, roughly a 67% drop, demonstrating that the authors recognize the need for dynamical constraints in interactive systems.

The ablation study further backs the design, since removing attention, interaction adaptation, or the VAE pushes aesthetic realism below the full model’s 96.8% (to 92.1%, 93.4%, and 91.3%, respectively). Still, the “aesthetic realism” metric only gauges how closely outputs resemble human-made art in VGG-16 feature space, so it reflects imitation of existing work rather than creativity or emotional resonance, which undermines the case that the system is a true artistic collaborator. The claim that user testing found the framework “perceived as creative” and engaging is likewise unsupported, as the methods describe no user study, participants, or survey data, and the interaction signals seem to be simulated. The authors do acknowledge that the system sacrifices control fidelity (correlation falling from 1.000 to 0.469) and that it was evaluated on a single dataset, but the “18.7% improvement” is also described inconsistently, labeled at different points as responsiveness, visual coherence, and a reduction in baseline responsiveness. In sum, the article works well as a proposal for stable, real-time generative art, but its assertions about creativity and user engagement are best read as promising hypotheses rather than proven findings___

Summary: the study by Chad et al. focuses on the understanding the perception of students on Chat-GPT, as a way to learn more about/implementation on the use of AI in education. The participants in the study were USAFA cadets, students studying to become part of the United States Air Force or United States Space Force, the curriculum is filled with various fields of engineering, and leadership skills. They were required to use Chat-GPT for their final essay, of which after they submitted (online) they were given the option to have a survey in relation to how the use of Chat-GPT affected their development of their output. The participants showed varying opinions regarding the use of the AI, though common themes show that Chat-GPT the students essentially understanded the capability of the AI in that while unbiased, required guidance from the students to be able to complete their task up to their standards.

Response: My main thought was about the sample used by the study, clearly a set of disciplined, educated, competent, honest individuals judging from their program and their code “We will not lie, steal, or cheat, nor tolerate among us any-one who does.” (page 3). These are the kinds of people I believe are best amplified by AI, they are smart enough to not use it as a crutch, and understands the AI’s capabilities clearly for them to use their own understanding to correct it. This study was during 2024, so AI now is far more capable compared to back then, but the point still stands that AI shouldn’t be used to replace human capability, instead rather to amplify it, and that as humans we should have the responsibility to keep ourselves capable as not to fall into laziness from automation, as with the statement in the paper “a conclusion consistent with the decades of human factors automation research that demonstrates automation does not replace the human but changes the way we work with technology” (page 10).

Title of reading selection: What’s next in AI: 7 trends to watch in 2026




Susanna Ray’s article, “What’s Next in AI: 7 Trends to Watch in 2026,” talks about how artificial intelligence is becoming a bigger part of our daily lives and how it can work together with people in different fields. The article discusses seven trends, including healthcare, scientific research, security, software development, and other developments that could change the way we live and work. Ever since AI became mainstream in today’s society, it has impacted all corners such as mentioned in the article: healthcare, research, software, and even the average everyday life of a person.

Microsoft states that AI has significantly improved all of these fields, backed up by statements such as “activity on GitHub reaching new levels in 2025. Each month, developers merged 43 million pull requests — a 23% increase from the prior year in one of the main ways teams propose and review changes to their code.” or “by Microsoft AI’s Diagnostic Orchestrator (MAI-DxO), which solved complex medical cases with 85.5% accuracy, far above the 20% average for experienced physicians.” including claims such as “AI will generate hypotheses, use tools and apps that control scientific experiments, and collaborate with both human and AI research colleagues,” in fields related to physics and science, with industry leaders like Aparna Chennapragada supporting this view by emphasizing the amplification of human workers, and Peter Lee and Mario Rodriguez further validate the technology's role in scientific discovery, and quantum computing.

It is to note that while AI has proved its capabilities and benefits, the article doesn’t talk about the negatives AI has on society. Such as job loss, privacy issues, inaccurate information, and people’s growing dependence on technology. The article is potentially biased towards AI as every voice belongs to a Microsoft or GitHub leader and the products highlighted, like Copilot, Azure, and Majorana 1, all come from Microsoft. No independent experts or skeptics weigh in, combined with the previous statistic regarding the improved accuracy in healthcare, leaving out that the physicians were unable to access books, colleagues, or other AI tools during the study—all tools doctors would typically use in their practice when diagnosing a patient.

Potential of bias aside, AI has and will continue to be a major part of humanity. There is no debate on the author’s idea that AI can make a positive difference in our lives, but it is to be remembered that it is only a tool and that human judgment, creativity, and decision making should never be completely replaced by technology, adding the risks in regards to your information and privacy upon interacting with large models such as Copilot, Azure, Gemini, Claude, ect. It is important that we agree that AI can be a tool that will help humans as long as we work together with it and not against it, and that we take responsibility in understanding the proper usage of AI, this is to say that artificial intelligence will be deeply integrated into our lives and we have to learn how to adapt alongside it for a better and more progressive future.
